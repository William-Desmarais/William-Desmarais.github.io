Project Detail

Solar Cell Antireflective Coating Optimization

An analysis of multilayer antireflective coatings designed to improve light and power transmission through solar cells.

← Back to portfolio

Project Description

This ELG 3106 project studied how two- and three-layer antireflection coatings affect the power transmitted to a photovoltaic cell. The design variables included the number of layers, their refractive indices, and their thicknesses.

The objective was to reduce reflection and maximize transmitted power across a useful portion of the solar spectrum. The report also examined why minimizing reflection at one central wavelength may not maximize the total power produced.

Hypotheses and simplifications

  • The solar cell was treated as a perfect absorber.
  • The coating was assumed to be lossless.
  • Each coating layer was assumed to be uniform and homogeneous.
  • Each layer thickness was assumed to be exactly one quarter of the center wavelength inside that material.

Simplified transmitted-power model

Ptransmitted = ∫ Esolar(λ) T(λ) dλ

The transmitted solar power was estimated by weighting the coating transmission T(λ) by the solar spectral irradiance Esolar(λ) over the selected wavelength range.

Method

MATLAB scripts modeled solar irradiance and calculated reflection and transmission through candidate coating structures. The Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) combined the optical response of each layer into a global response for the coating system.

For the initial coating designs, each layer was assumed to have exactly a quarter-wave optical thickness at the chosen center wavelength. In other words, the physical thickness depends on the refractive index of that material rather than being the same for every layer.

Quarter-wave thickness

di = λ0 / (4ni)

The optical path through layer i is one quarter of the center wavelength in that material.

Phase thickness

δi = (2π nidi) / λ

This phase term is used in the propagation matrix for each coating layer.

Lossless model

R(λ) + T(λ) = 1

With absorption neglected, reflected and transmitted power account for the incident power.

Spectral model

Reflectivity and transmitted power were evaluated over wavelength ranges including 400–1400 nm.

Matrix analysis

Propagation matrices were combined to evaluate the global multilayer response.

Optimization

Brute-force MATLAB searches compared thousands of refractive-index and layer configurations.

  • Computed reflection and transmitted power across the spectrum
  • Varied layer count, thickness, and refractive index
  • Compared two-layer and three-layer coating designs

Findings

The analysis showed that minimizing reflection at the central wavelength does not necessarily maximize total transmitted power. Broadband behavior must be considered because solar irradiance and coating performance change with wavelength.

The selected wavelength range also affects the calculated power. Extending the range beyond the useful solar-cell response does not automatically increase the physically meaningful power estimate.

Results

MATLAB searches identified refractive-index combinations that minimized central-wavelength reflection for two-layer and three-layer systems. The report also compared maximum-power solutions over broader wavelength ranges.

Engineering outcome A Transfer Matrix Method and MATLAB workflow for comparing multilayer photovoltaic coatings.

Two-layer optimization

The two-layer search varied the refractive indices of the coating materials while setting each thickness from the quarter-wave relationship. Candidate designs were compared using both their central-wavelength reflectivity and their integrated transmitted power over the selected solar spectrum.

Optimization focus Find a practical two-layer combination that reduces reflection over a useful wavelength range.

Three-layer optimization

The three-layer search introduced an additional refractive-index and thickness combination. This provided more freedom to shape the broadband response, but it also made the brute-force search much more computationally expensive than the two-layer case.

Optimization trade-off Additional layers can improve spectral control, but they increase the number of combinations that must be evaluated.

Lessons Learned

This project strengthened my understanding of electromagnetic modeling, optical propagation, the Transfer Matrix Method, and MATLAB-based parameter searches. I learned that minimum reflection at one wavelength and maximum integrated power are different optimization objectives.

The project also highlighted the computational cost of testing every possible three-layer combination and the value of more efficient optimization algorithms for future work.
